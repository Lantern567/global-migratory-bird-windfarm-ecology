# Wind-farm array orientation strongly shapes migratory-bird collision exposure with little energy loss
### — A geometric exposure screen of Western European offshore and onshore wind farms

---

## Abstract

Wind-energy expansion increasingly conflicts with migratory-bird conservation, yet the mitigation portfolio still leans on operational-phase measures. One pre-construction lever — the orientation of the turbine array relative to the migration axis — remains largely absent from that portfolio. We treat orientation as a quantifiable ecological design variable across 4,191 onshore wind farms and 55 offshore wind farms with measured migratory-bird directions in Western Europe (29 from VPTS weather radar, 26 from the Bauer grid). Four results emerge. Collision exposure is highly sensitive to orientation, with a median relative amplitude of 93–100% across three independent data sources. Orientations optimised purely for annual energy production diverge from the exposure optimum by a median of 40–48° and retain 90–99.8% avoidable exposure. Most of that avoidable exposure is recovered by a limited rotation: 14–18° captures half of the maximum benefit. And a 1% AEP budget delivers 96.9%, 84.2% and 47.6% median exposure reduction across the three groups. Array orientation therefore warrants routine inclusion in site-level screening as a low-cost complement to existing operational and blade-visibility measures.

**Keywords:** wind-farm array orientation; migratory-bird collision; geometric exposure; energy–ecology trade-off; wake model

---

## Introduction

Wind power anchors the clean-energy transition, yet its rapid expansion generates a heterogeneous set of environmental, social, economic, technical and legal impacts, and wildlife impact remains among the least resolved research gaps<sup>22</sup>. Collision mortality alone is estimated at 140,000–330,000 birds per year in the contiguous United States<sup>1</sup>, and a trait-based global assessment identifies migratory species as disproportionately vulnerable to collision, with the most sensitive species concentrated along coastlines and inland flyways<sup>2</sup>. Collision risk has moved from a local engineering nuisance to a first-order constraint on the ecological sustainability of wind energy.

Collision risk is not random. It is shaped by three space-time factors: flight height, migratory corridor, and turbine distribution. Flight height determines the fraction of a bird's flight spent inside the rotor-swept zone (roughly 20–150 m)<sup>3</sup>. GPS tracking of 1,454 individuals across 27 species places ≈13.6% of the European–North African airspace inside a height-sensitive band, with sensitive zones clustered along migration corridors and coastlines<sup>4</sup>. Migration direction is neither random nor plastic in the short term: long-lived raptors improve their compensation for wind drift as they age, through mortality selection<sup>23</sup>, showing that flyway geometry is stable enough to serve as a design reference. Band-model calculations combine these inputs into a collision probability<sup>5</sup>, and GPS records of 143 whimbrels crossing offshore wind farms confirm both substantial rotor-layer overlap and pronounced horizontal detouring<sup>6</sup>. The relative angle between an array and the local migration axis is therefore a first-order determinant of exposure.

Two mitigation tools now have quantitative anchors. A seven-year before-after-control-impact experiment at Smøla (Norway) showed that painting one of the three rotor blades black reduces bird fatalities by ≈70%<sup>24</sup>. Dynamic curtailment during high-migration nights avoids collisions in proportion to forgone generation: a recent Western-European estimate places the cost of avoiding 50% of potential collisions at 1.2% of annual energy production, and 90% at 7.6%<sup>10</sup>. Both act during operation, both require sustained investment in maintenance, real-time detection, or forgone generation<sup>7</sup>, and both leave the underlying barrier effect on migration<sup>8</sup> and the individual-scale avoidance mechanisms<sup>9</sup> untouched. A geometric variable that could be fixed at essentially zero cost during planning has, by contrast, gone unquantified.

That variable is the orientation of the array itself. When turbines are arranged in rows, birds crossing parallel to the rows can pass through the inter-row corridors with minimal exposure, whereas birds crossing perpendicular to the rows must traverse the rotor-swept plane once per row. Geometric exposure therefore follows a sin² law of the relative angle between array and migration direction (Fig. 1c). Preliminary layout-side work has started to widen row spacing to open corridors for seabirds<sup>25</sup>, but the more fundamental degree of freedom — the rotational orientation of the array — has not been tested as an independent ecological design variable. We test four propositions (Fig. 1): (i) migration direction makes exposure highly sensitive to orientation; (ii) the AEP-optimal orientation retains substantial avoidable exposure; (iii) a limited rotation captures most of the reducible exposure; and (iv) the ecological gains cost less than 1% of annual energy production.

We analyse 4,191 onshore wind farms and 55 offshore wind farms with measured migratory-bird directions across Western Europe (29 from VPTS weather radar, 26 from the Bauer grid). All four propositions hold. Throughout, we use five core concepts (Table 1) and distinguish three levels — collision risk (expected number of collisions), collision exposure (its un-calibrated approximation), and collision mortality (the measured, mortality-calibrated quantity). All exposure reductions reported here refer to the relative decline in geometric exposure, uncalibrated for species, flight height, and mortality; until such calibration is completed, the geometric angle serves for screening only.

Two Western-European settings anchor the analysis. Offshore, 171 wind farms have engineering-grade AEP orientation curves, of which 55 have measured migratory-bird directions (29 from VPTS weather radar, 26 from the Bauer grid). Onshore, per-turbine coordinates from OpenStreetMap (100,327 turbines) and wind-resource data from the Global Wind Atlas are fed through the Jensen wake model to compute AEP(θ) curves. Of the 5,869 farms for which AEP was computed, 4,191 fall within the coverage of the Bauer migratory-bird direction product and enter the trade-off analysis; the remaining 1,678 (mainly in the UK/Ireland, northern Europe/Baltic, and Iberia) fall outside this range and are excluded (Fig. 1a).

The migratory threat over the study region is quantifiable and substantial. VPTS weather radar records mean migration-season densities of 12–135 birds/km³ (spring 12–24, autumn 16–135), peak instantaneous densities of 1,287 birds/km³, and a maximum single-station autumn total flux of ≈825,000. Roughly half of the birds enter the rotor-swept height (rotor-layer fraction 0.23–0.71, median ≈0.51); Supplementary Fig. S1 shows that this height overlap is a systematic feature, providing the ecological premise for the geometric-exposure analysis that follows.

## Results

### Migration direction is structured, not diffuse, and drives strong orientation sensitivity

Migration direction has a stable principal axis rather than a diffuse distribution. Across 2,025 valid cells of the full Bauer grid, the median dominant spring direction is 51.5° (NE) and the autumn direction 234.9° (SW); directional concentration medians are 0.89 in spring and 0.76 in autumn (Fig. 2a, Fig. 2b). The two seasonal vectors are nearly antiparallel (a directed difference of ≈183°), but collision-exposure geometry is 180°-periodic, so the two axes differ by only ≈3.4° in exposure terms. One orientation therefore serves both seasons; spring and autumn do not form competing constraints requiring compromise. Unlike other ecological mitigation measures, orientation has a seasonally consistent, geometrically determined solution.

This directional structure translates directly into strong orientation sensitivity of exposure. Exposure is minimal at parallel alignment and maximal at perpendicular alignment, rising monotonically in between (Fig. 2d). The median directional sensitivity amplitude (rel, the relative exposure change achievable by rotation alone; Methods) reaches 99.9% onshore, 97.6% for offshore VPTS, and 93.4% for Bauer (Fig. 2e, Supplementary Fig. S2b); every site in all three groups exceeds a 50% amplitude, and every site also exceeds 80%. Array rotation alone spans nearly the full order-of-magnitude range of exposure — a lever that operational curtailment and blade-visibility measures reach only through continuous investment. Direction is therefore a first-order determinant on par with migration direction itself, not a secondary correction to risk.

The sensitivity is universal, not local. Three data sources differ in kind — a purely geometric wake model onshore, radar-measured flux offshore, and a gridded direction product — yet all converge on a 93–100% sensitivity range (Fig. 2g, Fig. 2h, Supplementary Fig. S2a, S2c). Cross-source robustness rules out a measurement artefact and identifies orientation sensitivity as a property of migration ecology, not of any single analytical pipeline. The planning implication is direct: array orientation belongs in every farm layout as a routine design variable, in the same way wake loss already does.

### Energy and ecology optima are systematically misaligned

Energy and ecology optima are systematically misaligned. The AEP-optimal orientation rarely coincides with the minimum-exposure orientation; the median angular offset is 48° onshore (interquartile range 29–60°), 40° for offshore VPTS (20–50°), and 45° for Bauer (30–68°) (Fig. 3b, Fig. 3c). The misalignment has a physical origin: the AEP-optimum is set by the wind resource, the exposure-optimum by the migration axis, and the two are independent. A 40–48° offset places the purely energy-optimised orientation close to the perpendicular-to-migration exposure peak rather than the trough. A single economic objective has, in effect, pushed ecological exposure toward the high end.

Exposure at the AEP-optimum runs an order of magnitude, and in some cases much more, above the achievable minimum. The exposure ratio (E(θ_econ)/E_min; Methods) reaches median values of ≈600× onshore, 19× for offshore VPTS, and 10× for Bauer (Fig. 3d). The three ratios themselves carry mechanistic information: the onshore sin² geometry approaches zero at parallel alignment, so E_min is very small and the ratio inflates to the hundreds; the offshore models integrate flux and flight height, which impose a non-zero exposure floor and hold the ratio near 10–19. Even under the most conservative offshore convention, one order of magnitude of exposure remains geometrically removable. The onshore 600× should be read as an upper bound of geometric exposure that is highly sensitive to angular error near the optimum (see Discussion), not as a precise mortality-rate gap.

Most exposure at the AEP-optimum is removable by re-orientation. The reducible exposure fraction (avoid = 1 − E_min/E(θ_econ); Methods) reaches a median of 99.8% onshore, 94.6% for offshore VPTS, and 90.0% for Bauer (Fig. 3g). More than 90% of exposure is therefore geometrically removable. What remains to determine is not whether to rotate, but by how many degrees and at what energy cost.

### Most of the benefit is captured within the first 20°

Most of the reducible exposure is captured within the first 20° of rotation. Starting from the AEP-optimal orientation, successive adjustments of 5°, 10°, 20° and 30° yield a capture fraction (frac_d, the share of the maximum reducible exposure recovered after a d° rotation; Methods) with medians of 15.3%, 29.9%, 54.8% and 72.9% onshore; 18.9%, 37.8%, 69.4% and 82.2% for offshore VPTS; and 14.5%, 28.9%, 52.9% and 73.6% for Bauer (Fig. 4a, Fig. 4b). The first 20° already deliver more than half of the benefit; the final stretch from Δθ80 to the full optimum contributes only the remaining ≈20%. This nonlinearity is not empirical: it follows directly from the sin² geometry, whose derivative with respect to angle is largest in the misaligned region and smallest at the trough.

A partial rotation captures most of the ecological benefit. Δθ50 and Δθ80 (rotations that capture 50% and 80% of the maximum reducible exposure) are 17° and 29° onshore, 14° and 25° for VPTS, and 18° and 30° for Bauer (Fig. 4d, Fig. 1e), all far smaller than the full misalignment (48°, 40°, 45°). Benchmarked at half the benefit, the required rotation is only about one-third of the full offset (17° vs 48°, 14° vs 40°, 18° vs 45°). A moderate compromise orientation therefore captures most of the ecological value without demanding a full rotation to the ecological optimum.

The pattern holds farm by farm, not only in the mean. The share of farms capturing more than 50% of the maximum reducible exposure within ≤20° is 58.3% onshore, 69.0% for VPTS, and 53.8% for Bauer, rising to 73.9%, 79.3%, and 69.2% at ≤30° (Fig. 4g). More than half of the farms therefore reach the half-benefit threshold within 20°, and seven to eight in ten reach it within 30°. The high yield of small-angle adjustment is a farm-level regularity, not a distributional artefact of the median.

### Ecological gain is largely free of AEP cost

Ecological gain is largely free of AEP cost. Under a 1% AEP cap, the mean realised AEP loss is 0.52% onshore, 0.38% for offshore VPTS, and 0.49% for Bauer, and the corresponding mean exposure reductions are 82.6%, 75.0%, and 50.1% (medians 96.9%, 84.2%, and 47.6%) (Fig. 5b, Fig. 5c, Fig. 5h). Across all three groups the mean AEP cost stays under 0.6%, yet purchases 50–83% mean exposure reduction (median 48–97%). This asymmetry between paid AEP and delivered exposure reduction is the central empirical result of the study.

The energy–exposure frontier is asymmetric by construction. Under a 1% AEP budget, the median share of the maximum reducible exposure captured is 98.0% onshore, 88.0% for VPTS, and 74.5% for Bauer (Fig. 5d, Fig. 5e). The geometry of the frontier explains this: AEP is flat near its optimum, because any optimum has zero first derivative, whereas exposure is steep in the misaligned region (the nonlinear capture curves of the previous subsection). A small AEP sacrifice therefore corresponds to a large exposure reduction — ecological benefit clusters in the early degrees of rotation, and energy loss accumulates only at large angular deviations. The two are staggered, making ecological benefit at low energy cost a structural feature rather than a site-specific accident.

Ecological benefit saturates rapidly beyond a 1% AEP budget. Raising the AEP cap from 2% to 5%, the fraction of farms with no additional exposure benefit is 85.8% onshore, 93.1% for VPTS, and 69.2% for Bauer; the marginal ecological return of a looser energy constraint has approached zero (Fig. 5f, Fig. 5g). This delineates a clear optimal investment window: the great majority of ecological benefit is concentrated within a ≈1% energy concession, and further concessions yield very low marginal return. Orientation optimisation is therefore best treated not as a continuous "energy for ecology" trade-off but as a one-off adjustment completed within ≈1% AEP.

The gradient across the three groups (onshore 98.0% > VPTS 88.0% > Bauer 74.5%) reflects the resolution of the direction data and the exposure models rather than generation performance or site quality: the onshore purely geometric model is the most sensitive to orientation and has the most concentrated benefit, while the Bauer grid product carries the coarsest spatial resolution and the most dispersed benefit. As migratory-direction data improve in precision, the achievable benefit at other sites should approach the onshore level.

## Discussion

Array orientation is a design lever whose ecological externality far exceeds its energy externality. Rotating the array before construction imposes no additional investment, land, or material, yet reduces the median geometric collision exposure of onshore Western-European farms by ≈97% (median 96.9%) and of offshore VPTS farms by ≈84% (median 84.2%) within a 1% AEP concession; the effect is more modest for offshore Bauer-grid farms (median 47.6%). The lever complements rather than replaces existing measures. Seven-year field experiments show that painting one blade black reduces avian fatalities by ≈70% during operation<sup>24</sup>, and dynamic curtailment can avoid 50% or 90% of potential collisions at 1.2% or 7.6% AEP<sup>10</sup>. Orientation acts during planning; painting and curtailment act during operation; together they form a mitigation chain from drawing board to control room. More generally, the wind-energy system contains design variables whose ecological externalities dominate their energy externalities, and orientation is the cheapest and most direct of these.

Evidence across the four propositions is not uniformly strong. Directional structure and misalignment are supported at the geometric-screening level and rely on no behavioural assumptions: measured directional distributions, monotonic exposure–orientation relationships, and systematic offsets between energy and ecological optima. The limited-rotation and low-cost claims, by contrast, depend on the shape of the AEP(θ) curve, whose numerical values are sensitive to the direction convention and the wake model. Amplifying the orientation-penalty term of the wake model by 1.5×, 2×, and 3× lowers the median exposure reduction from 97% to 92%, 82%, and 62%, placing the uncertainty interval of the central number in the range of ≈60–95%. The Jensen decay constant is a second-order effect: shifting α from 0.075 to 0.05 lowers the median only from 97% to 95%. Neither amplification nor α reconciles the residual gap between onshore orientation sensitivity (≈2.8%) and offshore FLORIS (8.8%); that gap reflects the wake models themselves and the direction-data conventions.

The scope of applicability is clear. Orientation optimisation acts only on new farms whose array orientation has not yet been fixed at the planning stage. For farms already built with a fixed layout, this lever no longer exists, and one must rely on dynamic operational curtailment<sup>10</sup> or blade-visibility measures<sup>24</sup>. The offshore direction conventions matter: the Bauer full-grid spring median is 51.5° (NE), the 26-farm offshore subset is 57.8° (NE), and the VPTS rotor-layer spring median is 348.9°, ≈69° from the Bauer subset — this convention gap explains the difference between VPTS (84.2%) and Bauer (47.6%) exposure reductions. We treat Bauer as the primary source and VPTS as the upper-bound reference from direct rotor-layer observation. Coarse flyway overlays such as the CAFF Arctic migratory-bird flyways cover 99.9% of wind sites, but each flyway spans 27 million–105 million km², too coarse for farm-level hazard identification; identifying true hazard zones requires flux, flight-height, species, and turbine-distribution data.

Two steps remain before this screening result extends to wider regions and higher precision. The 88 offshore wind farms of East Asia lie on the East Asian–Australasian flyway, but no public migratory-bird flight-direction product covers that region, and ERA5 meteorological wind direction is not a substitute for observation — the mean angular difference is 88° and only 13% of observations fall within 30°<sup>13</sup>. Geometric exposure must also be calibrated with species-level flux and mortality before it becomes a quantitative collision-mortality reduction. Neither step alters the core judgement that orientation is a low-cost, effective ecological design lever; both refine the precision and reach of the numbers.

### Geometric exposure model

The geometric exposure model links array orientation θ (0–180°) to the spring and autumn migratory-bird flight directions φ_spring and φ_autumn: E(θ) = 0.5 × [sin²(θ − φ_spring) + sin²(θ − φ_autumn)]. When the array is parallel to the migration direction, sin²(0°) = 0, and birds pass through the inter-row corridors with minimal exposure; when perpendicular, sin²(90°) = 1, and birds traverse the rotor-swept plane once for each row crossed, maximising exposure. The boundary values are determined by geometry and do not depend on behavioural assumptions. This model outputs geometric exposure (a screening assumption), uncalibrated for flight height, avoidance rate, and mortality, and does not constitute a measured collision-mortality reduction; the offshore VPTS/Bauer models additionally incorporate flux and flight height and are closer to collision exposure, but are likewise uncalibrated for mortality. Therefore the "exposure reductions" in this paper all refer to geometric-exposure decline rates, and the onshore and offshore exposure models are not directly comparable in absolute value.

### Offshore AEP (FLORIS)

Each of the 171 offshore wind farms has an AEP curve over 18 orientation angles (0–170°, 10° steps), from the preceding stage of FLORIS wake simulation<sup>18</sup>, engineering-grade precision data.

### Onshore AEP (Jensen)

Onshore has no ready-made FLORIS data, and is computed by the following pipeline: the OSM Overpass API<sup>19</sup> obtains 100,327 turbine coordinates → DBSCAN clustering (eps = 3 km, min_samples = 3) yields 5,954 wind farms → PCA extracts the principal layout-axis angle (mean explained variance 87.7%); turbine parameters are provided by OSM tags (covering 58% of turbines), with missing values filled by spatial nearest-neighbour from the Bauer database<sup>12</sup>; after removing micro/vertical-axis turbines with hub height or rotor diameter below 20 m, 5,869 farms remain. Wind resource is provided by Global Wind Atlas<sup>20</sup> Weibull parameters (interpolated by a logarithmic wind profile at the actual hub height). AEP is computed with the Jensen analytic wake model<sup>21</sup> (onshore decay constant α = 0.075, multiple-wake RSS superposition): 59 wind-speed bins pointwise-integrate the Weibull probability density to obtain wake-free energy, wake efficiency is weighted by Weibull probability at four representative wind-speed bins (4/8/12/16 m/s), convolved with 36 inflow wind directions (10° resolution), and swept over 18 orientation angles. The 5,869 farms (93,964 turbines) have a total optimal-orientation generation of about 547 TWh (capacity factor 28.1%), of which the 4,191 farms entering the trade-off analysis generate about 373 TWh annually.

### Metrics of orientation optimisation

We report five per-farm metrics throughout, each answering a specific question about how orientation regulates exposure. The **directional sensitivity amplitude** rel = (E_max − E_min)/E_max is the relative magnitude of exposure change achievable by rotation alone. The **reducible exposure fraction** avoid = 1 − E_min/E(θ_econ) is the fraction of exposure removable by re-orientation from the energy optimum. The **exposure ratio** E(θ_econ)/E_min is the factor by which the energy-optimum exceeds the minimum achievable exposure. The **ecological adjustment angle** Δθ is the smallest rotation from the energy optimum to the ecological optimum, and its Δθ50/Δθ80 variants are the smallest rotations that capture 50%/80% of the maximum reducible exposure. The **capture fraction** frac_d is the share of the maximum reducible exposure recovered after rotating by d° from θ_econ.

### Energy–ecology trade-off definition

θ_econ = argmax AEP(θ) (the economic-optimal orientation); θ_eco = argmin E(θ), subject to AEP(θ) ≥ (1 − budget) × AEP(θ_econ) (the ecological-optimal orientation). AEP cost = realised loss / AEP_max; exposure reduction RR = [E(θ_econ) − E(θ_eco)] / E(θ_econ) × 100%; ecological adjustment angle = the minimum rotation from θ_econ to θ_eco. Four AEP budgets: 0.5%, 1%, 2%, 5%, corresponding to low-cost, balanced, conservation-priority, and maximum scenarios.

---

## Conclusion

Across 4,191 onshore wind farms and 55 offshore wind farms with measured migratory-bird directions in Western Europe, array orientation is a first-order, low-cost ecological design lever: a ≈1% AEP concession delivers a median 96.9% geometric collision-exposure reduction onshore and 84.2% for offshore VPTS farms, with a more modest 47.6% for offshore Bauer-grid farms. Because it acts before construction, orientation complements — it does not replace — dynamic operational curtailment<sup>10</sup> and blade-visibility measures<sup>24</sup>. Upgrading geometric exposure to a species-level, mortality-calibrated collision-mortality estimate is the natural next step. It will refine the precision of the numbers without overturning the core judgement that orientation is a low-cost, effective ecological design lever.
